ART-ISMS

How art styles began, what they look like, and how each one grew out of the last, with three real works for every style.

45,000 years · 40 styles · 120 works · 2 languages See the timeline

How many art styles are there?

There is no official number. Lists of named movements run to several hundred, most of them offshoots, regional schools or short-lived groups, and historians disagree on where one ends and the next begins. This atlas maps 40 that changed how art is made.

Periods, movements, techniques and cultural traditions are different kinds of category. Broad labels such as “African”, “Indigenous” and “Islamic” cover many distinct peoples, places and histories, and are used here as entry points, not as single styles.

01

Prehistoric Art

c. 40,000–3,500 BCE Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia

Charcoal, ochre and the first images.

Before writing, people painted animals and handprints deep inside caves and carved small figures from stone, bone and ivory. Nobody knows exactly why: ritual, hunting magic, storytelling and teaching are all proposed. What is certain is that image-making is far older than cities, farming or the alphabet.

  • Chauvet Cave paintings, Unknown Aurignacian artists, c. 36,000 years ago. Ardèche, France. Photo shows a replica in the Anthropos Pavilion, Brno; the cave is closed to the public
  • Venus of Willendorf, Unknown Gravettian artist, c. 28,000–25,000 BCE. Natural History Museum, Vienna
  • Lascaux cave paintings, Unknown Magdalenian artists, c. 17,000 years ago. Dordogne, France

02

Ancient Egyptian Art

c. 3100–30 BCE Nile Valley

Sacred and political order, made to last for eternity.

For three thousand years Egyptian artists worked to strict rules. Figures combine a profile head, a frontal eye and frontal shoulders so every part is shown in its most recognisable view. Size signals rank, not distance. Most of it was made for tombs and temples, to keep the dead and the gods supplied forever.

  • Narmer Palette, Unknown, c. 3100 BCE. Egyptian Museum, Cairo
  • Bust of Nefertiti, Thutmose (workshop), c. 1345 BCE. Neues Museum, Berlin
  • Nebamun hunting in the marshes, Unknown, c. 1350 BCE. British Museum, London

03

Indigenous Arts: Three Traditions

ancient–present Pacific Northwest, American Southwest, Central Australia

Not one style: place, ceremony, kinship and living continuity.

“Indigenous art” is not a style but thousands of living traditions, each rooted in a particular land, language and ceremony. The three examples here come from the Haida of the Pacific Northwest Coast, the Hopi of the American Southwest and the Western Desert of Central Australia. Their forms carry kinship, law and stories, and artists continue to practise and renew them today.

  • Raven transformation mask, Charles Edenshaw (Haida), 19th century. Pitt Rivers Museum, Oxford
  • Katsina figure, Hopi artist, late 19th century. Brooklyn Museum, New York
  • Warlugulong, Clifford Possum Tjapaltjarri, 1977. National Gallery of Australia, Canberra

04

Greek & Roman Classical

c. 800 BCE–400 CE Mediterranean

Ideal proportion, civic meaning and observed bodies.

Greek sculptors moved from stiff, frontal figures to bodies that shift their weight and seem to breathe. They chased ideal proportions and wrote them down as rules. Rome copied, spread and engineered this language across an empire. Many “Greek” statues we know are Roman marble copies of lost bronzes, and most were once brightly painted.

  • Discobolus (Discus Thrower), Roman marble copy after Myron, c. 140 CE copy of a c. 460–450 BCE bronze. Bronze original lost. Shown: the Lancellotti copy, Palazzo Massimo, Rome
  • Parthenon frieze: horsemen, Workshop of Phidias, c. 443–437 BCE. British Museum, London
  • Augustus of Prima Porta, Unknown Roman sculptor, early 1st century CE. Vatican Museums

05

African Sculptural Traditions

c. 500 BCE–present Many cultures across the continent

Sculpture for authority, memory, ritual and protection.

Africa has thousands of distinct cultures, not one style. For well over two thousand years, from Nok terracottas to the cast heads of Ife and the brass plaques of Benin, sculpture has served kings, ancestors, healing, law and celebration. Many objects were made to be used, worn or activated in performance, not looked at in a museum. In the early 1900s, African works seen in Paris helped reshape European modernism.

  • Ife Head, Yoruba artist, 14th–early 15th century. British Museum, London
  • Benin plaque: warrior and attendants, Edo artist, c. 1540–1570. This plaque: Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York. Many others are in European museums
  • Nkisi nkondi (power figure), Kongo artist, c. 1880–1900. Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York

06

Byzantine Art

330–1453 Eastern Roman Empire

Images as spiritual presences, not windows.

When the Roman capital moved to Constantinople, art turned from describing the body to revealing the divine. Figures became flat, frontal and weightless, set against shimmering gold. Mosaics and icons were meant as windows to heaven, which is why arguments over images (Iconoclasm, 726–843) could become state crises.

  • Christ Pantocrator (Sinai), Unknown icon painter, 6th century. Saint Catherine’s Monastery, Sinai
  • Basilica of San Vitale, Unknown builders and mosaicists, consecrated 547. Ravenna, Italy
  • Deësis mosaic, Hagia Sophia (detail: John the Baptist), Unknown, c. 1261. Hagia Sophia, Istanbul

07

Islamic Art

7th century–present Spain to India, and beyond

Writing, geometry and pattern carry meaning.

“Islamic art” is a broad label for the art of many regions and centuries, not one style. Across that range, artists developed a shared language of geometry, vegetal arabesque and calligraphy. Religious spaces avoid figurative images, so pattern itself carries meaning; courtly manuscripts, by contrast, are full of figures and stories. Early mosaics drew on Byzantine craftsmen.

  • Folio from the Blue Qur’an, Unknown, 9th–10th century. Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (this folio)
  • Ardabil Carpet, Maqsud of Kashan, 1539–1540. Victoria and Albert Museum, London
  • Court of the Lions, Alhambra, Nasrid builders, 14th century. Granada, Spain

08

Chinese Landscape & Literati Painting

10th century–present China

Mountains, water and the cultivated brush.

From the 10th century Chinese painters made mountains and rivers the main subject, centuries before Europe treated landscape as more than background. Instead of one fixed viewpoint, the eye travels through the scroll. From the Yuan dynasty on, scholar-painters valued brushwork, poetry and personal expression over likeness, joining painting, calligraphy and verse into one practice.

  • Travelers Among Mountains and Streams, Fan Kuan, c. 1000. National Palace Museum, Taipei
  • Six Gentlemen, Ni Zan, 1345. Shanghai Museum
  • Lotus and Ducks, Bada Shanren (Zhu Da), c. 1696. Freer Gallery of Art, Washington

09

Romanesque

c. 1000–1150 Western Europe

Stone stories for pilgrims.

As pilgrimage routes and monasteries spread across Europe, churches grew massive, with thick walls, round arches and small windows. Over their doors sculptors carved the Last Judgment for a largely illiterate public, using exaggerated, expressive figures. The Bayeux Tapestry shows the same storytelling drive in embroidery.

  • Bayeux Tapestry, Unknown embroiderers, c. 1070s. Bayeux Museum, Normandy (on loan to the British Museum, London, until July 2027)
  • Last Judgment tympanum, Autun, Gislebertus, c. 1130. Autun Cathedral, France
  • Central tympanum, Vézelay, Unknown, c. 1120–1132. Basilica of Sainte-Marie-Madeleine, Vézelay

10

Gothic

c. 1140–1450 Western Europe

Height, coloured light and graceful naturalism.

Pointed arches and flying buttresses let church walls become thin frames for stained glass. Sculpture and painting grew more graceful and human; by 1300 painters such as Giotto were giving figures real weight and emotion. The name began as a Renaissance insult that linked the style to the “barbarian” Goths.

  • Notre-Dame de la Belle Verrière (Blue Virgin window), Unknown, c. 1150–1220. Chartres Cathedral, France
  • Visitation, Reims Cathedral, Unknown, c. 1230. Reims Cathedral, France
  • Wilton Diptych, Unknown, c. 1395–1399. National Gallery, London

11

Italian Renaissance

c. 1400–1600 Florence, Rome, Venice

Perspective, anatomy and the rebirth of antiquity.

Florentine artists rediscovered Greek and Roman models and paired them with mathematics. Linear perspective, demonstrated by Brunelleschi and described by Alberti in 1435, made a flat wall look like a window. Painters studied anatomy and light, and artists rose from craftsmen to celebrated individuals.

  • The Birth of Venus, Sandro Botticelli, c. 1484–1486. Uffizi Gallery, Florence
  • The Last Supper, Leonardo da Vinci, 1495–1498. Santa Maria delle Grazie, Milan
  • The School of Athens, Raphael, 1509–1511. Apostolic Palace, Vatican

12

Northern Renaissance

c. 1420–1580 Flanders, Netherlands, Germany

Oil paint and every last detail.

North of the Alps, painters perfected oil glazes that could render velvet, metal and reflections with uncanny precision. They cared less about classical theory and more about close observation, hidden symbolism and everyday life. Printmaking spread their images across Europe.

  • Arnolfini Portrait, Jan van Eyck, 1434. National Gallery, London
  • The Garden of Earthly Delights, Hieronymus Bosch, c. 1490–1500. Museo del Prado, Madrid
  • The Hunters in the Snow, Pieter Bruegel the Elder, 1565. Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna

13

Indian Miniature Painting

c. 1500–1900 Mughal, Rajput and Pahari courts

Fine brushwork, saturated colour, history and devotion.

Small, intensely detailed paintings made for albums and manuscripts at Indian courts. Mughal workshops blended Persian, Indian and European ideas into vivid histories and portraits, often with several artists working on one page. Rajput and Pahari painters turned to Krishna, poetry and music, with bold flat colour and lyrical landscapes.

  • Shuja’at Khan pursuing Asaf Khan on the Ganges, from the Akbarnama, Tulsi the Elder and Jagjivan, Mughal imperial workshop, c. 1590–1595. Victoria and Albert Museum, London
  • Krishna and Radha in a Pavilion, Mewar painter, 1719. Art Institute of Chicago
  • Savant Singh and Bani Thani as Krishna and Radha in a Palace Garden, Attributed to Nihal Chand, c. 1760. Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco

14

Mannerism

c. 1520–1600 Italy, Spain, France

After perfection, elegance and strain.

Once Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael had seemed to “solve” painting, younger artists exaggerated instead. Necks and limbs stretched, poses twisted, colours turned cool and acidic, and space became crowded and unclear. Long dismissed as decadent, Mannerism is now read as a deliberately sophisticated style.

  • Madonna with the Long Neck, Parmigianino, 1534–1540. Uffizi Gallery, Florence
  • An Allegory with Venus and Cupid, Agnolo Bronzino, c. 1545. National Gallery, London
  • The Burial of the Count of Orgaz, El Greco, 1586–1588. Church of Santo Tomé, Toledo

15

Baroque

c. 1600–1750 Italy, Spain, Flanders, Dutch Republic

Drama, darkness and a spotlight.

The Catholic Church, answering the Reformation, wanted art that would grip ordinary viewers. Baroque artists used violent contrasts of light and dark, diagonal motion and theatrical moments, and fused painting, sculpture and architecture into total spectacles. In the Protestant Dutch Republic the same skills went into portraits, everyday scenes and still lifes for a new middle-class market.

  • The Calling of Saint Matthew, Caravaggio, 1599–1600. San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome
  • Ecstasy of Saint Teresa, Gian Lorenzo Bernini, 1647–1652. Santa Maria della Vittoria, Rome
  • Las Meninas, Diego Velázquez, 1656. Museo del Prado, Madrid

16

Ukiyo-e

c. 1670–1900 Edo-period Japan

Woodblock prints of the floating world.

Ukiyo-e, “pictures of the floating world”, were affordable colour woodblock prints of actors, courtesans, travel and legends for the townspeople of Edo. Each print needed a designer, a carver and a printer. When they reached Europe in the mid-1800s their flat colour, bold outlines and cropped views changed the course of Western art.

  • The Great Wave off Kanagawa, Katsushika Hokusai, c. 1831. Many impressions; e.g. Metropolitan Museum, British Museum
  • Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge and Atake, Utagawa Hiroshige, 1857. Many impressions
  • Three Beauties of the Present Day, Kitagawa Utamaro, c. 1793. Many impressions; e.g. Museum of Fine Arts, Boston

17

Rococo

c. 1715–1780 France, then Europe

Pastels, flirtation and ornament.

After Louis XIV’s heavy grandeur, French aristocrats wanted intimacy and fun. Rococo brought pastel colour, curling asymmetrical ornament and scenes of love in parks. Enlightenment critics soon attacked it as frivolous, which helped open the door to Neoclassicism.

  • The Swing, Jean-Honoré Fragonard, c. 1767. Wallace Collection, London
  • Pilgrimage to Cythera, Antoine Watteau, 1717. Louvre, Paris
  • Madame de Pompadour, François Boucher, 1759. Wallace Collection, London

18

Neoclassicism

c. 1760–1830 France, Italy, Britain

Clear line and civic virtue.

Excavations at Herculaneum and Pompeii and the ideas of the Enlightenment made ancient Greece and Rome fashionable again. Neoclassical artists rejected Rococo frivolity for sharp outlines, calm surfaces and stories of sacrifice and duty. The style served revolutionaries and then Napoleon.

  • Oath of the Horatii, Jacques-Louis David, 1784. Louvre, Paris
  • The Death of Marat, Jacques-Louis David, 1793. Royal Museums of Fine Arts, Brussels
  • Grande Odalisque, Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, 1814. Louvre, Paris

19

Romanticism

c. 1790–1850 Germany, France, Britain, Spain

Feeling, storms and the sublime.

Against Neoclassical reason, Romantic artists valued emotion, imagination and the overwhelming power of nature. They painted shipwrecks, revolutions, mountains and solitary figures before vast landscapes. Colour and loose brushwork returned as tools of feeling.

  • Liberty Leading the People, Eugène Delacroix, 1830. Louvre, Paris
  • The Slave Ship, J. M. W. Turner, 1840. Museum of Fine Arts, Boston
  • Wanderer above the Sea of Fog, Caspar David Friedrich, c. 1818. Hamburger Kunsthalle

20

Realism

c. 1840–1880 France, Russia, United States

Ordinary people, painted big.

Realists refused both classical ideals and Romantic drama. They painted peasants, workers and village funerals at the size once reserved for kings and gods. Courbet said he could not paint an angel because he had never seen one. Photography, announced in 1839, sharpened the question of what painting is for.

  • The Stone Breakers, Gustave Courbet, 1849. Destroyed in 1945
  • The Gleaners, Jean-François Millet, 1857. Musée d’Orsay, Paris
  • The Third-Class Carriage, Honoré Daumier, c. 1862–1864. Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York

21

Impressionism

c. 1860–1890 Paris and northern France

Light, weather and the passing moment.

Rejected by the official Salon, a group of painters held their own exhibition in 1874. A critic mocked Monet’s Impression, Sunrise and the name stuck. Using portable tube paint, they worked outdoors in quick broken strokes to catch changing light, and painted modern Paris: cafés, boulevards, railways and leisure.

  • Impression, Sunrise, Claude Monet, 1872. Musée Marmottan Monet, Paris
  • Bal du moulin de la Galette, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, 1876. Musée d’Orsay, Paris
  • The Ballet Class, Edgar Degas, 1873–1876. Musée d’Orsay, Paris

22

Post-Impressionism

c. 1886–1905 France

Structure, symbol and emotional colour.

Not a group but a label, coined by Roger Fry in 1910, for painters who pushed past Impressionism in very different directions. Seurat systematised colour into dots, Cézanne rebuilt nature from solid planes, Van Gogh and Gauguin made colour carry feeling and symbol. Almost every 20th-century movement starts here.

  • The Starry Night, Vincent van Gogh, 1889. Museum of Modern Art, New York
  • Mont Sainte-Victoire and Château Noir, Paul Cézanne, c. 1904–1906. Artizon Museum, Tokyo
  • Vision of the Sermon (Jacob Wrestling with the Angel), Paul Gauguin, 1888. Scottish National Gallery, Edinburgh

23

Art Nouveau

c. 1890–1910 Paris, Brussels, Vienna, Barcelona

Organic line, everywhere.

Art Nouveau tried to erase the line between fine art and design. Flowing plant-like curves appeared on posters, jewellery, metro entrances and whole buildings. Inspired by nature and Japanese prints, it was the first international style aimed at modern mass production, and it faded quickly after 1910.

  • JOB cigarette paper poster, Alphonse Mucha, 1896. Poster, many copies
  • The Kiss, Gustav Klimt, c. 1907–1909. Belvedere, Vienna
  • Paris Métro entrance, Hector Guimard, c. 1900. Paris

24

Fauvism

c. 1905–1910 France

Colour set free.

At the 1905 Salon d’Automne, critic Louis Vauxcelles called the painters “fauves”, wild beasts. Matisse, Derain, Vlaminck and others used pure, unmixed colour that had nothing to do with what things really looked like. The movement lasted only a few years but opened the way to abstraction.

  • Woman with a Hat, Henri Matisse, 1905. San Francisco Museum of Modern Art
  • Charing Cross Bridge, André Derain, 1906. Musée d’Orsay, Paris
  • Le Bonheur de vivre (The Joy of Life), Henri Matisse, 1905–1906. Barnes Foundation, Philadelphia

25

Expressionism

c. 1905–1930 Germany, Austria, Norway

Inner feeling over outer reality.

Expressionists distorted shape and colour to show what life felt like, not what it looked like. Germany’s Die Brücke (1905) and Der Blaue Reiter (1911) pushed raw colour, jagged forms and anxiety about the modern city. The Nazis later branded much of this work “degenerate”.

  • The Scream, Edvard Munch, 1893. National Museum, Oslo
  • Street, Berlin, Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, 1913. Museum of Modern Art, New York
  • Self-Portrait with Physalis, Egon Schiele, 1912. Leopold Museum, Vienna

26

Cubism

c. 1907–1925 Paris

Many viewpoints at once.

Picasso and Braque, building on Cézanne and on African and Iberian sculpture, broke objects into faceted planes and showed them from several angles at once. Analytic Cubism (about 1909–1912) reduced colour to browns and greys; Synthetic Cubism (from 1912) brought in collage and bright flat shapes. Gris, Gleizes and Metzinger spread the language. It ended five centuries of single-point perspective as the default.

  • Still Life before an Open Window, Place Ravignan, Juan Gris, 1915. Philadelphia Museum of Art
  • Les Baigneuses (The Bathers), Albert Gleizes, 1912. Musée d’Art Moderne de Paris
  • En Canot (In the Boat), Jean Metzinger, 1913. Lost; seized by the Nazis in 1937

27

Futurism

1909–1944 Italy, Russia

Speed, machines and noise.

Launched by F. T. Marinetti’s manifesto on the front page of Le Figaro in 1909, Futurism worshipped speed, cars, factories and the city, and called for museums to be destroyed. Painters borrowed Cubist fragmentation to show motion over time. Its glorification of war and later ties to Fascism remain part of its story.

  • Unique Forms of Continuity in Space, Umberto Boccioni, 1913. Bronze casts, e.g. MoMA and Tate
  • Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash, Giacomo Balla, 1912. Buffalo AKG Art Museum
  • Dynamism of a Car, Luigi Russolo, 1912–1913. Centre Pompidou, Paris

28

Suprematism & Constructivism

c. 1913–1934 Russia / Soviet Union

Pure geometry, then art for a new society.

Kazimir Malevich wanted painting reduced to pure feeling through basic shapes, starting with a black square. Constructivists such as Tatlin and Rodchenko took geometry out of the studio and into engineering, posters, typography and architecture for the new Soviet state. Stalin’s Socialist Realism shut both down in the early 1930s.

  • Black Square, Kazimir Malevich, 1915. Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow
  • Monument to the Third International (Tatlin’s Tower), Vladimir Tatlin, 1919–1920. Unbuilt; models and reconstructions
  • Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge, El Lissitzky, 1919–1920. Poster, Civil War era

29

Dada

1916–1924 Zürich, Berlin, Paris, New York

Anti-art in answer to a mad war.

Born at the Cabaret Voltaire in Zürich in 1916, Dada answered the slaughter of the First World War with absurdity. If reason had produced the trenches, Dadaists would trust chance, nonsense and provocation instead. Readymades, photomontage and sound poems questioned what art is, and that question has driven art ever since.

  • Fountain, Marcel Duchamp; photo by Alfred Stieglitz, 1917. Original lost; 1964 replicas in several museums
  • Cut with the Kitchen Knife, Hannah Höch, 1919–1920. Neue Nationalgalerie, Berlin
  • L’Œil cacodylate (The Cacodylic Eye), Francis Picabia, 1921. Centre Pompidou, Paris

30

De Stijl / Neoplasticism

1917–1931 Netherlands

Universal balance through line, plane and primary colour.

Founded around Theo van Doesburg’s magazine De Stijl in 1917, the movement sought a universal visual language. Piet Mondrian called his version Neoplasticism: only horizontal and vertical lines, the three primaries, and black, white and grey. The same rules shaped furniture and houses. Van Doesburg’s embrace of the diagonal split the two men in the mid-1920s.

  • Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow, Piet Mondrian, 1930. Kunsthaus Zürich
  • Red and Blue Chair, Gerrit Rietveld, 1918–1923. Many examples (shown: NGV, Melbourne)
  • Counter-Composition XIII, Theo van Doesburg, 1925–1926. Peggy Guggenheim Collection, Venice

31

Bauhaus

1919–1933 Weimar, Dessau, Berlin

A school that redesigned everyday life.

Walter Gropius founded the Bauhaus in Weimar in 1919 to unite art, craft and industry. Students passed through a shared foundation course, then workshops in metal, weaving, furniture, typography, theatre and architecture. The school moved to Dessau in 1925 and closed under Nazi pressure in 1933; its teachers carried its ideas to the United States and beyond.

  • Bauhaus Building, Dessau, Walter Gropius, 1925–1926. Dessau, Germany
  • Wassily Chair (Model B3), Marcel Breuer, 1925–1926. Still in production
  • Triadic Ballet, Oskar Schlemmer, 1922. Premiered in Stuttgart; costumes now in the Staatsgalerie Stuttgart

32

Art Deco

c. 1920–1940 Paris, New York, Shanghai, Rio, worldwide

Machine-age glamour.

Named after the 1925 Paris exhibition of decorative arts, Art Deco mixed Cubist geometry, ancient Egyptian motifs (Tutankhamun’s tomb was opened in 1922) and the streamlined look of cars and ocean liners. It was luxurious, commercial and global, from New York skyscrapers to the Shanghai Bund.

  • Chrysler Building, William Van Alen, 1928–1930. New York
  • Christ the Redeemer, Paul Landowski, Heitor da Silva Costa, Albert Caquot, Gheorghe Leonida, 1922–1931. Rio de Janeiro, Brazil
  • Self-Portrait in the Green Bugatti, Tamara de Lempicka, 1929. Private collection

33

Latin American Modernisms

c. 1920s–1960s Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Cuba and beyond

Modernism remade through local histories and politics.

Artists who had absorbed Cubism and Surrealism in Europe came home to build their own modern art. Mexican muralists put revolution and labour on public walls; Brazilian modernists proclaimed “cultural cannibalism”, devouring European ideas to make something Brazilian; Frida Kahlo turned her body and heritage into a personal mythology. The muralists’ scale and politics later influenced painters in the United States.

  • The Two Fridas, Frida Kahlo, 1939. Museo de Arte Moderno, Mexico City
  • Detroit Industry Murals, Diego Rivera, 1932–1933. Detroit Institute of Arts
  • Abaporu, Tarsila do Amaral, 1928. MALBA, Buenos Aires

34

Surrealism

1924–1966 Paris, then worldwide

Painting the dreaming mind.

André Breton’s 1924 manifesto, drawing on Freud, set out to free the unconscious. Some Surrealists used automatic drawing and chance; others painted impossible dreams in hyper-precise detail. Its uncanny combinations of ordinary things spread into film, fashion and advertising. The movement is usually dated to Breton’s death in 1966.

  • The Treachery of Images, René Magritte, 1929. Los Angeles County Museum of Art
  • The Persistence of Memory, Salvador Dalí, 1931. Museum of Modern Art, New York
  • The Storm (Black Landscape), Yves Tanguy, 1926. Philadelphia Museum of Art

35

Abstract Expressionism

c. 1943–1965 New York

Huge canvases, raw gesture.

After the Second World War the centre of the art world shifted from Paris to New York. Action painters such as Pollock and de Kooning made the act of painting itself the subject; Colour Field painters such as Rothko used vast areas of colour to envelop the viewer. Pollock drew partly on Navajo sand painting and on the Mexican muralists. It was the first American movement with worldwide influence.

  • Autumn Rhythm (Number 30), Jackson Pollock, 1950. Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
  • No. 61 (Rust and Blue), Mark Rothko, 1953. Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles
  • The Liver Is the Cock’s Comb, Arshile Gorky, 1944. Buffalo AKG Art Museum

36

Pop Art

c. 1955–1972 London, New York

Soup cans, comics and celebrity.

Pop artists took their images from advertising, comics, packaging and film stars. Against the private angst of Abstract Expressionism, they offered cool, ironic, mass-produced looking pictures, often made with commercial methods such as silkscreen. It began in Britain in the mid-1950s and exploded in New York in the early 1960s.

  • Marilyn Diptych, Andy Warhol, 1962. Tate, London
  • El Cap de Barcelona (Barcelona Head), Roy Lichtenstein, 1992. Port Vell, Barcelona
  • Just what is it that makes today’s homes so different, so appealing?, Richard Hamilton, 1956. Kunsthalle Tübingen

37

Minimalism

c. 1959–1975 United States

What you see is what you see.

Minimalists stripped art of gesture, emotion and illusion. Their works are often industrially made boxes, bricks or light tubes, arranged in simple repeating orders. Frank Stella summed it up in 1964: “What you see is what you see.” The viewer’s body and the room became part of the work.

  • Untitled (Stack), Donald Judd, 1980 (stacks from 1965). Tate, London
  • Equivalent VIII, Carl Andre, 1966. Tate, London
  • “monument” for V. Tatlin, Dan Flavin, 1964–1990 (this version 1969–1970). This version: National Gallery of Art, Washington

38

Conceptual Art

c. 1965–1980 United States, Europe, Japan

The idea is the artwork.

Conceptual artists took Duchamp’s question to its limit: if an idea makes something art, the idea itself can be the work. Instructions, texts, photographs, performances and systems replaced the crafted object. Sol LeWitt wrote in 1967 that “the idea becomes a machine that makes the art.”

  • One and Three Chairs, Joseph Kosuth, 1965. Museum of Modern Art, New York
  • Grapefruit, Yoko Ono, 1964. Artist’s book, first published in Tokyo
  • Today series (date paintings), On Kawara, from 1966. Many collections

39

Postmodernism

c. 1970s–1990s United States, Europe

Quotation, appropriation, identity and irony.

Postmodern artists doubted that art could be original or pure. They borrowed, quoted and re-photographed images from film, advertising and art history, and asked who gets represented, and by whom. Irony, spectacle and questions of identity replaced the modernist search for an essential form.

  • Untitled Film Stills, Cindy Sherman, 1977–1980. Museum of Modern Art, New York
  • Puppy, Jeff Koons, 1992. Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (since 1997)
  • Untitled (Cowboy), Richard Prince, 1989. Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (one print)

Contemporary Art

c. 1990–present Global

A plural field, everywhere at once.

Since around 1990 there has been no dominant movement. Artists move freely between installation, painting, video, performance, participation and digital media, and the art world has become truly global, with biennales and fairs on every continent. Shared concerns include identity, history, labour, technology, the environment and the market itself.

  • Infinity Mirror Rooms, Yayoi Kusama, 1965–present. Many versions worldwide
  • Maman, Louise Bourgeois, 1999 (this cast 2001). This cast: Guggenheim Museum Bilbao; others worldwide
  • A Subtlety, or the Marvelous Sugar Baby, Kara Walker, 2014. Domino Sugar Factory, Brooklyn (temporary)
